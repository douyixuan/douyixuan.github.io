## Introduction

Philosopher Brian Hedden argues for “time-slice rationality”: rationality may be better evaluated at particular moments rather than as a property of a person extended across an entire lifetime. Useful for IELTS philosophy and social-science reading.

## Vocabulary

**rationality** — reasoning sensibly from evidence; **skeptical** — doubtful; **circumstance** — condition surrounding an event; **temporally extended** — existing across time; **locus** — central location of something; **streamlined** — made simpler and more efficient; **epistemology** — philosophy of knowledge; **anticipate** — expect in advance; **sunk cost** — an unrecoverable past cost; **deliberate** — think carefully; **counterproductive** — producing the opposite of the intended effect; **hindsight bias** — seeing past outcomes as more predictable after they occur; **reassessment** — evaluating again; **contrarian** — opposing a prevailing view.

## Reading comprehension

**1. What does “time-slice rationality” mean?**

{{< answer >}}
Rationality should primarily be judged from the evidence and reasons available to a person at a particular moment.
{{< /answer >}}

**2. Why are past and future selves compared to teammates?**

{{< answer >}}
Different temporal versions of a person can have different information and interests, so cooperation across time resembles coordination among different people.
{{< /answer >}}

**3. How does the gym-membership example illustrate the theory?**

{{< answer >}}
A decision that once made sense can later cease to be rational when circumstances change; past expenditure alone should not determine the present choice.
{{< /answer >}}

**4. Why might some apparent hindsight bias actually be rational reassessment?**

{{< answer >}}
New evidence available after an event can rationally change one's assessment, so not every retrospective change of belief is a cognitive error.
{{< /answer >}}
