## English retelling / 英文故事式复述（非原文）

据 NASA Glenn Research Center 的[风洞实验](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/1901-wind-tunnel-tests/)和[自行车实验](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/1901-bicycle-tests/)两篇资料独立复述。未转载原文或图片。

<section class="ielts-source-text" aria-label="English retelling, not the original article">
<p>The Wright brothers had a problem that another glide would not solve. In 1901, their glider lifted with only about a third of the force their calculations predicted. Were the numbers they had borrowed reliable? To find out, they turned to a vehicle they knew well: a bicycle.</p>
<p>On the streets of Dayton, Ohio, the moving bicycle supplied the airflow. An extra wheel on its handlebars held a little wing and a flat plate. The two pieces tried to twist the wheel in opposite directions. When their turning effects balanced, the wheel stopped at an angle. That angle was the reading. It suggested that the small wing was generating less lift than the published figures had led the brothers to expect.</p>
<p>But the road gave them clues, not a controlled test. Back indoors, a fan moved air through a box. Now the brothers could put a small wing in the airflow without having to ride anywhere. They made one instrument for lift and another for drag. After a first round of trials, they narrowed the field to about thirty designs.</p>
<p>The useful comparison was between two wings that differed in just one feature. If their readings changed, the brothers knew which feature to investigate, instead of guessing among several. The measurements went into a better glider in 1902 and helped with the propellers for their 1903 aircraft. The bicycle did not give them all the numbers they needed. It showed them which numbers to measure for themselves.</p>
</section>
