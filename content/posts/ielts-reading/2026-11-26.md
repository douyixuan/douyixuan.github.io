## English retelling: When It Snows Beneath the Sea

英文故事式复述，非原文。根据 [NOAA Ocean Exploration 的科普原文](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/marinesnow/) 改写。原文发表于 2013 年 10 月 21 日，无个人署名。下潜镜头是讲述视角，不是对某次观测的逐帧记录。

Imagine watching a live feed from a camera descending into the Gulf of Alaska. The scene is dark until the lights pick out dozens of white specks. They drift through the beam like snow, but nothing here is frozen. To find out what the camera is seeing, you have to go back up to the sunlit surface.

There, tiny organisms called phytoplankton use light to grow. Material from them and from microbes starts to sink. On the way down, it meets other particles: waste, bits of dead animals and even fine sediment. Some of these pieces stick together. The larger clumps fall faster, but a journey from the surface to the deep ocean can still take weeks.

Now imagine that the camera comes back the next day and sees far fewer specks. That doesn't necessarily mean the surface produced less plankton. Animals can eat the flakes before they reach the bottom, decomposition can break them down, and currents can carry them out of view. What passes the camera is the result of what was made above and what happened along the way.

At the bottom, this slow delivery matters. Some animals catch the particles as they pass; others pick through what settles on the seabed. Researchers have found usable carbon and nitrogen in the flakes. Think of a shipwreck sitting nearby: a dusting may begin to collect within a few years, long before it is buried from sight. A single flake is easy to miss, but the steady supply helps feed animals far below the sunlit waters where its journey began.
