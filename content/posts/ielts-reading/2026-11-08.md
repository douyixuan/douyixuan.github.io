Track three contrasts: pre-training versus fine-tuning, calculated descriptors versus published experimental literature, and research evidence versus claims of universal performance.

## Introduction

预测新分子的性质，往往需要昂贵的合成与实验。本文介绍 CheMeleon 如何先从大量分子的可计算特征中学习，再用少量针对性数据适应新任务。阅读时注意：“少量数据”说的是哪一个阶段？模型减少实验需求，并不等于不再需要实验。

## Vocabulary

- **scant** — 少量的、不足的。
- **trial-and-error** — 反复试验、试错。
- **myriad** — 大量的、各种各样的。
- **laborious** — 费时费力的。
- **synthesize** — 合成；本文指制造化合物。
- **winnow down** — 筛选缩小范围。
- **descriptor** — 描述符；用于描述分子某一特征的数值或指标。
- **solubility** — 溶解度。
- **viscosity** — 黏度；流体对流动的阻力。
- **fine-tune** — 微调；用特定任务的数据进一步训练已有模型。

## Reading strategy

给每个数字加一个标签：**一百万是分子数、1,600 是描述符数、58 是测试案例数**。再把动词分成两列：pre-train 学习通用模式，fine-tune 适应目标性质。不要把“目标任务只需少量数据”误读成“整个系统只用少量数据训练”。

## Expression practice

以下例句为自拟，并非原文引文。

**from scratch** — 从零开始。
Example: Reusing a trained model can reduce the need to learn every pattern from scratch.

**on the other hand** — 另一方面，用来引出对照。
Example: Large experiments provide direct evidence; simulations, on the other hand, can help narrow the search.

## Review

用三句英文复述：模型在预训练时学什么、微调阶段为什么能使用较少数据、文章提供了哪些证据。避免把比较测试的胜出写成“对所有分子和所有性质都准确”。

### How to predict the behavior of molecules from scant data

**A machine-learning approach called CheMeleon provides a practical way to develop new drugs or other molecules with much less detailed, expensive experimentation.**

David L. Chandler | Department of Chemical Engineering  
Source: [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2026/how-to-predict-molecule-behavior-with-scant-data-1106)  
November 6, 2026

<section class="ielts-source-text" aria-label="Original English article">

<p>Discovering new molecules for specific uses is a major driver of progress in a wide variety of industries, whether it’s pharmaceutical companies looking for new drugs, or electronics or energy companies seeking new materials with particular properties. But it’s also largely a trial-and-error process, because there is not yet any way to perfectly predict how a given chemical compound will behave in terms of the myriad different properties and responses materials can exhibit.</p>

<p>Recent progress has been made on using artificial intelligence and machine learning to supplement or speed up laborious calculations based on physical principles that can at least provide some initial guidance on which compounds might be worth synthesizing and testing. Now, a new method developed at MIT shows that when there are few data on some technologically relevant property, such as ligand affinities for a recently discovered cellular target for a potential drug, this approach can outperform other existing methods for winnowing down the field of possible molecules that might be worth looking at.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/jcisd8/article-abstract/66/17/10692/5250516/Deep-Learning-Foundation-Models-for-Low-Data?redirectedFrom=fulltext" target="_blank">findings were reported</a> in the Sept. 14 issue of the <em>Journal of Chemical Information and Modeling</em>, in a paper by MIT graduate student Akshat Shirish Zalte and recent graduate Jackson Burns PhD ’26, Research Scientist Charlles Abreu, Professor William Green, and three researchers from the chemical company BASF.&nbsp;</p>

<p>An earlier version of the paper has been online since last year, and the open-source model the team developed, called CheMeleon, has already found regular use by several pharmaceutical companies. CheMeleon was built using ChemProp, an open-source machine-learning package also developed and maintained by the Green group at MIT, and anyone can download CheMeleon through ChemProp and fine-tune it for their own purposes.</p>

<p>Earlier this year, there was a contest organized by OpenADMET for trying to predict the properties of a variety of new molecules that had not previously been synthesized, says Green, who is the Hoyt Hottel Professor of Chemical Engineering. “It was a big contest, a lot of people participated,” he says. When the winners were announced, it turned out that nine of the top 20 entries had used CheMeleon in their process. “So then we started getting all kinds of inquiries from the pharma companies about this, but our paper had just been submitted; it wasn’t even peer-reviewed or anything yet.”</p>

<p>While the paper went through that review process, its influence was already spreading. At least one pharma company, Green says, “sent a message to all their people, and said ‘Anytime you’re modeling a property, start with CheMeleon, that’s our new company policy’” when making initial decisions about which molecules to pursue.</p>

<p>The key to CheMeleon was pre-training a machine-learning model with detailed data about a million different molecules, so that it gets to learn correlations between the structure of a given molecule and how it behaves with respect to a catalog of about 1,600 descriptors that have been developed by chemists over decades. CheMeleon can then be fine-tuned to predict well-known properties like solubility and viscosity, to more esoteric properties such as bioactivity or reactivity, with a certain protein or enzyme.</p>

<p>Those million molecules are a minuscule fraction of the innumerable ways that atoms can bond together, but they provide enough examples so that once the AI system has been trained on them, it can work out on its own many underlying patterns and relationships, and then apply those to a new, unknown molecule that’s presented to it.</p>

<p>“Most of today’s deep-learning models require more data than traditional methods,” says Zalte. Some successful models use&nbsp;extensive data from the published literature in chemical journals, he says. “We don’t look at the literature at all. We just calculate a bunch of molecular descriptors like molecular weight or number of oxygens, among others.” Then predicting those descriptors forms the basis for their pre-training task.</p>

<p>CheMeleon is a pre-trained foundation model. Inspired by the well-known foundation models in language such as ChatGPT and Claide, which essentially are developed by feeding them massive amounts of information from the internet, CheMeleon uses molecular descriptors of a million molecules from PubChem, a large, freely available database of chemical information maintained by the U.S. National Institutes of Health.&nbsp;</p>

<p>With classical expert-driven models, on the other hand, specialists in a field manually input detailed knowledge about their subject. In developing these classical models, he says, for example, people know from experiments that a certain property of interest, say solubility, may relate to particular descriptors of the same molecules, “like molecular weight, or the number of oxygens that are present, or how branchy or bulky the molecule is.”&nbsp;</p>

<p>With this team’s approach, they can start with an existing neural network architecture and train it to learn underlying principles. “We’re not training it for a specific property, but as a general model that understands some level of chemistry, so that even if you fine-tune it with just a few data points for the task you care about, you can still get some good performance,” he says. That’s thanks to the fact that the model is “not having to learn everything from scratch.”</p>

<p>To verify its usefulness, “We compared our model not only against other foundation models that are out there, but also with classical models leveraging expert-designed principles,” Zalte says. Comparing its performance across 58 test cases, “we see that our model comes out on top.”</p>

<p>Green adds, “The CheMeleon foundation model drastically reduces the number of molecules that need to be tested experimentally to accurately predict the properties of new molecules. This is a huge advantage, since synthesizing and testing a large number of molecules is slow and expensive."</p>

<p>Besides the MIT members, the team also included Jochen Sieg, Christian Feldmann, and Miriam Mathea, researchers at BASF SE in Germany. The work was supported by the Machine Learning for Pharmaceutical Discovery and Synthesis consortium, along with fellowship support from U.S. Department of Energy and MathWorks.</p>
